How Manufacturers Can Digitize Forms and Approval Workflows?

INSIGHTS•24 June 2026•6 min read

Many manufacturers still rely on paper or spreadsheets for everyday processes such as purchase requests, quality checks, equipment maintenance, and leave applications. These methods can seem manageable until a form goes missing, an approver is unavailable, or someone needs to find an old record quickly.

Across several departments or sites, these small delays can slow daily operations and make it harder to see what's happening. This article walks through which forms to digitize first, how to design approval workflows, and how to roll out the change with your teams.

Short Answer

Manufacturers can digitize their workflow quickly by:

  • Rebuilding high-volume or high-delay forms to eForms
  • Route it according to their SOPs
  • Test it with one team
  • Expand the workflow to more departments or work sites

Which forms to Digitize first?

Start with forms that are submitted often, wait on several approvers, or are regularly needed for reporting. Avoid your most complex process at first and try with a simpler form that requires an easier workflow.

FormTypical usersWhat digitizing improves
Leave and overtime requestsAll employees, supervisors, HRFaster approvals and easier attendance reporting
Purchase requestsRequesters, managers, procurement, financeClear approval status and spending records
Equipment maintenance requestsOperators, maintenance teamsEasier issue tracking and a searchable equipment history
Quality inspection recordsQC inspectors, production supervisorsConsistent data and quicker follow-up on defects
Nonconformance reportsQuality, production, engineeringClear ownership of corrective actions
Engineering change requestsEngineering, production, qualityBetter review tracking across departments
Safety incident reportsEmployees, supervisors, safety teamsFaster reporting and easier trend analysis

To choose your first form, list your current forms and score each one on three questions:

  • Volume: How often is it submitted?
  • Delay: How long does it usually wait for approval or follow-up?
  • Data value: Would better data from this form help managers make decisions?

Start with a form that scores well on all three but has a simple approval path.

How to design approval workflows

A good digital workflow reflects how decisions should be made, not just how the paper form has always moved. Before building it, confirm who needs to review each request, why they need to review it, and what happens if the answer is no.

When designing your workflow, keep these principles in mind:

  • Remove unnecessary steps. Keep an approval only if that person is making a real decision.
  • Use conditions. Route requests based on factors such as amount, department, site, or request type.
  • Assign roles instead of names. Approval paths are easier to maintain when people change jobs.
  • Plan for absences. Set backup approvers and decide when overdue requests should be escalated.
  • Define what happens after rejection. Make it clear whether a request is closed or returned for changes.
  • Keep a full record. Capture who acted, when they acted, and any comments they added.

How to roll out digital forms

1. Choose an owner

Assign someone to each process who can make decisions and update the form when requirements change.

2. Run a small pilot

Begin with one form and one team, ideally including both office and shop-floor users if they share the process.

3. Set a clear switch date

Decide when the paper version stops, so teams don't have to manage two systems for long.

4. Train people on the task

Show each group what it needs to do, such as submitting, approving, or checking status.

5. Plan for shop-floor access

Confirm that operators and inspectors can reach the form during their shifts, including those without their own computer.

6. Collect feedback and adjust

Ask users what slowed them down, then fix confusing fields or approval steps before expanding.

7. Expand in stages

Add more teams, sites, and forms once the pilot is working, and reuse what you've learned.

What to measure

Record a baseline before the pilot starts, so you can show what changed. A few simple measures are usually enough:

MeasureWhat it shows
Approval cycle timeHow long a request takes from submission to final decision
Waiting time by stepWhere approvals are getting stuck
Rejected or returned formsWhether fields or instructions are causing errors
Record retrieval timeHow quickly someone can find a past form
Digital adoptionHow many submissions now use the digital form

Once the data builds up, you can also look for operational patterns. For example, maintenance requests may reveal which machines fail most often, while quality records may show which defects keep returning.

Common mistakes to avoid

Copying the paper form exactly. Remove fields nobody uses, and take the chance to simplify the process.

Adding too many approvers. Every extra step adds a possible delay, so keep only the reviews that serve a clear purpose.

Designing without shop-floor input. A form built only by the office may not fit how operators and inspectors actually work.

Forgetting about absent approvers. Without backup approvers or escalation rules, one person's absence can stop the whole process.

Running paper and digital indefinitely. Parallel systems split your records and make the data harder to trust.

Collecting data nobody reviews. Decide who will use each report and what decisions it should support.
